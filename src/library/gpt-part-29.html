About This Paper

Graviton Pressure Theory never argues against a measurement. Every result stands, and every part of the mechanism must pass it. This paper gathers the measurements that bear on whether space is a medium that presses, rather than an empty geometry whose straightest paths bodies follow.

Its claim is exact and limited. Every result in gravity and light reads as a medium, exactly, with the same numbers; and in each case the geometric reading leaves unnamed what touches what. [Argued] It does not claim that the measurements prove a medium. A result that two readings give with the same number cannot choose between them by its number. What it can show is that the medium reading loses nothing, and names what the other leaves out.

The paper does five things. It sets out how a result is read (Section 1). It recalls how much of this physics began with a medium (Section 2). It sets the measured results beside their two readings (Section 3). It states what a medium must survive, with GPT’s answer to each (Section 4). And it gathers the experiments that could show a medium beyond a geodesic, where GPT expects something the standard account does not (Section 5). The classroom demonstrations often cited for or against a pressure account are read again at the end (Section 6).

This is the second edition; what changed from the first is listed at the end. Claims are marked by their status, as the key shows.

29.1 How a Result Is Read

29.1.1 Three kinds of difference

Two accounts can differ in three ways, and only two of them can be settled by an experiment. [Argued]

  • The same outcome, read differently. Both accounts give the same number, and differ in what they say causes it. Free fall, the bending of light, the slowing of clocks and the turning of Mercury’s orbit are all here: GPT reproduces the standard numbers and names a different mechanism (Parts 5 and 8).12 No experiment that measures only the outcome can choose between them; the choice rests on mechanism, on which account names what touches what. Sections 2 and 3 are about results of this kind.

  • An effect the standard account does not address. GPT expects something the standard account is silent on, or expects to be zero: a held body’s weight depending on its structure, a spinning body leaving an imprint beyond its shape, a clock’s slowing depending on its internal order. An experiment can look for these.

  • A different number. The two accounts expect different values for the same measurement. The clearest is the behaviour of gravity where the inflow’s acceleration falls to about \(1.2\times10^{-10}\) m/s\(^2\): GPT’s relaxation boundary, against the standard account’s dark matter (Part 9).3

29.1.2 How a test is read

A test of the whole mechanism and a test of one of its parts say different things. [Argued] An outcome test measures a result directly, such as two materials falling alike to one part in \(10^{15}\);4 it constrains every account, whatever its parts. A subset test models some of the parts, and shows only that those parts alone do not produce the result. So each test in Section 5 is stated with four things: what GPT expects, what the standard account expects, what has been measured, and what result would count against GPT.

29.2 Where Physics Began with a Medium

Much of the physics of gravity and light was first worked out as the behaviour of a medium, and the medium was set aside for economy, not refuted. [Argued]

  • Newton wrote to Bentley in 1693 that a body acting on another at a distance, through nothing, was to him “so great an absurdity” that no competent thinker could fall into it.5 He gave the law, and left the cause open.

  • Pushing gravity. Fatio in 1690 and Le Sage in the eighteenth century proposed that gravity is a push: bodies driven together by a flux from all sides, each shading the other (Part 1).67

  • Light in moving water. Fresnel derived, from a medium partly carried by moving matter, that light in water flowing at speed \(v\) is carried along by \(v(1-1/n^2)\). Fizeau measured it in 1851.8

  • Maxwell’s equations came from a model of a medium filling space, with magnetism as its swirl. The speed of its waves, computed from bench measurements of electricity and magnetism, matched the speed of light (Part 28).910

  • The ether wind. Michelson and Morley in 1887 looked for the Earth’s motion through the medium as a change in light’s speed with direction, and found none.11 Lorentz’s theory of 1904, with a medium at rest and rods and clocks changed by motion through it, predicts this null result, and everything special relativity predicts.12 After 1905 the medium was dropped because no experiment could find the frame it rests in.

  • The rotating ether. Sagnac titled his paper of 1913 “the luminiferous ether demonstrated”: light sent both ways round a turning loop returns at different times.13 Michelson and Gale detected the Earth’s own rotation this way in 1925.14

  • Einstein’s ether. In 1920 Einstein said at Leiden that under General Relativity “space without ether is unthinkable”, an ether with physical properties but no parts that can be followed through time.15

  • Gravity as an index and as a river. Eddington in 1920 described the bending of light by the Sun as refraction in a medium of raised index.16 Painlevé in 1921 and Gullstrand in 1922 wrote the field outside a mass as flat space through which something flows inward at the escape speed.1718

  • Gravity in a fluid. Unruh showed in 1981 that sound in a flowing fluid obeys the same equations as light near a black hole, with a horizon where the flow outruns the sound.19

29.5 Where GPT Expects Something Different

29.5.1 The relaxation boundary

Hypothesis

GPT expects: far from a mass, where the inflow’s acceleration falls to about \(a_0 \approx 1.2\times10^{-10}\) m/s\(^2\), the medium sent on by the mass is brought back into step, and gravity changes its form. The boundary lies at \(r = \sqrt{GM/a_0}\): about 7,000 times the Earth’s distance from the Sun for the Sun, and within the disc of a galaxy for a galaxy, where it gives the measured rotation from the visible mass alone (Parts 9 and 16).22 The standard account expects Newton’s law at every distance, with dark matter added where the visible mass falls short.

Galaxies support it. [Argued] The 153 rotating galaxies of a large survey follow one relation between the acceleration its visible mass sets and the acceleration its rotation shows, with the change at \(a_0\).23 Clusters of galaxies, and the third peak of the background light, are where dark matter fits and GPT’s account is still owed.

Pairs of stars are the direct test, and the results conflict. [Argued] Pairs orbiting each other at thousands of times the Earth’s distance from the Sun reach accelerations near \(a_0\), and the Gaia satellite has measured their motions. One analysis finds their accelerations about 1.4 to 1.5 times Newton’s below \(10^{-10}\) m/s\(^2\);24 another, of a larger sample, finds Newton’s law and excludes the standard modified-gravity law by sixteen standard deviations.25 The disagreement turns on how unseen companions and measurement errors are handled, and is not settled.

How a pair’s boundary behaves inside the galaxy’s own flow is still owed. [Open] The galaxy’s inflow at the Sun is about \(2\times10^{-10}\) m/s\(^2\), close to \(a_0\), so a pair of stars near the Sun is not alone. GPT must say how the two flows combine before a pair of stars can test it cleanly.

Hypothesis

GPT’s own test: direction. A mass moves through the medium, and its counterflow is carried with it: the relaxation boundary lies closer ahead than behind (Part 16). So pairs of stars whose separation lies along their motion through the medium should depart from Newton’s law at a smaller separation than pairs lying across it. The standard account, and modified gravity, expect no difference with direction. Sorting the Gaia pairs by direction is a re-reading of data that already exist. Its expected size is owed.

What would count against it: [Argued] pairs of stars following Newton’s law at every separation and in every direction, once GPT’s account of the embedded boundary is in hand; or a dark-matter particle found in the amounts the galaxies need.

29.5.2 A held body’s weight and its structure

Hypothesis

GPT expects: falling and holding are different acts. In a fall the medium’s delivery builds up as motion in every particle at once, and nothing passes from one part of the body to another, so every body falls alike. Held, the delivery must pass through the structure, layer by layer, into the support. The route depends on the structure and on how it is aligned to the inflow, and the share reaching the support may depend on it too: part could leave with the medium the body sends on [Hypothesis] (Part 15).26 The standard account expects a held body’s weight to be set by its mass alone, apart from its height and the air it displaces.

Which is right has not been tested. [Open] The test is plain, with one care. Equal mass cannot be set by weighing, since that would assume the answer, so the same pieces are weighed in each arrangement: one set of sheets stacked flat, stood upright, and assembled into a honeycomb, compared on a mass comparator against a reference that does not change. Rearranging the pieces does not change their mass, beyond the small energy of the arrangement. A solid block cast from the same sheets can be compared the same way, before and after. Figure 29.1 shows what it must control:

  • Height. Weight falls by about three parts in ten million for every metre a body’s centre of mass is raised, so the centres of mass must match to a fraction of a millimetre.

  • Air. A honeycomb displaces more air than a solid block, so the weighing is done in vacuum.

  • Surface. A honeycomb has far more surface, and surfaces hold water and gas; the pieces are baked, or made with equal surface area.

  • The support. Each piece is weighed on a balance whose pan rests on a mechanical support and on one whose pan is held by magnetic suspension, in case the kind of support matters. Non-magnetic, uncharged material keeps the scale reading one thing.

The standard expectation is far below what a comparator can see. [Argued] Rearranging a structure changes the energy it holds, in its surfaces and internal stresses, by a few joules at most for a kilogram, which alters its mass by less than one part in \(10^{15}\). A difference at one part in \(10^{10}\), at matched height in vacuum, would therefore lie beyond anything the standard account expects.

A crystal turned.

Weighing a strongly layered crystal in two orientations tests whether its total weight depends on alignment; a torsion balance tests whether its centre of gravity moves from its centre of mass as it is turned. Highly oriented pyrolytic graphite, whose layers carry heat and charge hundreds to thousands of times better along them than across, is the natural specimen. Poynting and Gray searched in 1898 for a directional action between two quartz crystals and found none at the sensitivity of their time.27 Alignment alone shows nothing where it has been tested: [Argued] torsion pendulums carrying some \(10^{23}\) aligned electron spins show no extra gravitational effect.28

A vibration across a vacuum.

With two crystals in a row and the first set vibrating, and the crystals screened electrically and magnetically and held at one temperature, what remains across a vacuum in the standard account is the gravity of the moving mass, which can be calculated. Any excess at the vibration’s frequency would be a transmission through the medium beyond gravity’s ordinary form.

What would count against it: [Argued] no difference with structure or orientation at one part in \(10^{10}\) would bound how much of the delivery any structure can send anywhere other than the support. It would not touch the account of falling.

29.5.3 A spinning body’s imprint

Hypothesis

GPT expects: a spinning body sets the medium around it turning, and the medium carries the spin itself, so a spinning body leaves an imprint beyond what its shape accounts for (Part 16). In Newton’s account a uniform sphere’s field outside it does not depend on its spin; in General Relativity the spin adds a dragging of order \(GJ/c^2r^3\), measured around the Earth by Gravity Probe B,29 and far too small to see around a laboratory rotor.

The size of the imprint is still owed. [Open] The test is a torsion balance or gravimeter beside a symmetric rotor, compared with the rotor at rest and spun each way. Any signal that follows the spin and not the shape would be the imprint. What would count against it: [Argued] once GPT derives the imprint’s size, no signal at that size. Until then a null result bounds the imprint without testing it.

29.5.4 Clocks and their internal order

Every kind of clock tested is slowed by the same factor: [Argued] nuclear and atomic clocks, optical clocks compared over a third of a metre, and the decay of muons, to the precision of each test.30313233 In GPT the factor is the medium streaming past every exchange, and is common; [Hypothesis] each clock’s own rate is set by its structure (Part 18).34 No test we know of has compared the slowing of one clock as its internal order is changed: [Open] the same clock, raised the same height, with its internal order altered. The standard account expects no change. What would count against it: [Argued] a change in the slowing as the order is changed would count against the factor being common; no change, to the clock’s precision, is what GPT’s common factor expects, and bounds any part that depends on order.

29.5.5 Shading at alignments

Gravity is not measurably shielded by matter [Argued] (Part 15). A small shading by absorption remains in GPT’s account at the scale of the atom, and possibly where bodies line up. Eclipses and close alignments are the places to look, [Hypothesis] with gravimeters recording through them and the expected tidal signal removed. The standard account expects nothing beyond the tide. What would count against it: [Argued] no signal beyond the tide through alignments, at the precision of superconducting gravimeters, sets an upper bound that GPT’s account of absorption must fit beneath.

29.5.6 Rhythms and the redistribution of delivery

Where inside a structured body the medium’s delivery lands may depend on its order, and living rhythms may amplify it [Hypothesis] (Part 13).35 Five tests follow:

  • sealed vessels under constant conditions, screened for the lunar tidal periods of 12.42 hours, 13.66 days and 14.77 days, with light and temperature removed;

  • the Moon replaced by a one-tonne lead sphere 0.3 metres away, giving about \(7\times10^{-7}\) m/s\(^2\), moved on a 12.42-hour cycle;

  • the settling of starch grains in plant cells, compared with the order of the cells around them;

  • biological records matched against superconducting gravimeter records of the same place;

  • the activation record: people’s daily rhythms against the Moon’s tidal phase at their birth, with the season and cultural effects removed.

The standard account expects only the effects of light, temperature and the tide’s direct forcing. What would count against it: [Argued] sealed vessels showing no lunar-period rhythm once light, temperature and the tide’s forcing are removed, and no response to the lead sphere’s cycle, would count against delivery being redistributed and amplified by living rhythms.

Conclusion

Every measured result in gravity and light reads as a medium, exactly, with the same numbers: the field outside a mass is an inflow at the escape speed, clocks are slowed by the medium streaming past them, light is refracted by its raised index, gyroscopes are turned by its swirl, and light presses as a wave of it. In each case the geometric reading gives the same number and leaves unnamed what touches what. Much of this physics began with a medium, and the medium was set aside because no experiment of the time could find it, not because one refuted it. A medium must also survive what was once counted against it; four of those constraints are met, two have proposals awaiting test, and two are owed. And there are experiments that could show a medium beyond a geodesic: gravity’s change of form near \(a_0\), with GPT’s own test by direction in data that already exist; a held body’s weight against its structure; a crystal turned; a spinning rotor; a clock with its internal order changed. Table 29.4 gathers them, each stated so that a result can count against GPT.

It separates three kinds of difference (the same outcome read two ways, an effect the standard account does not address, a different number), of which only the last two can be settled by experiment.

  • Added: where physics began with a medium (Section 2); the measured results in gravity and in light and fields, each with its medium reading and what the standard reading leaves unnamed (Tables 29.1 and 29.2); and what a medium must survive, with GPT’s answer and status for each (Table 29.3).

  • The demonstrations are read again (Table 29.5), each first stating what both accounts agree on. Kept as readings of the same outcome: the robot arm, the magnet and washer, the balloon in a falling lift, tension, the comb, the gyroscope, the steel plate. Withdrawn as divergences, because their outcomes are measured to be the same under both accounts: the tilt of a quartz-and-foam body in a fall, the earlier motion of dense over loose bodies, the delayed response of hollow spheres in a fall, the rotation of an L-shaped body in a vacuum, apparent mass from heating beyond \(\Delta E/c^2\), rising holding cost as fatigue, a magnet’s flip changing the gravity around it, and coherent shielding beyond the measured bounds.

  • Restated as tests: the angular weight shift as a crystal’s centre of gravity turned in a torsion balance; hollow against solid as the weighing of the same pieces in different arrangements, held rather than dropped; corridors of crystals as a vibration across a vacuum; the rotating mass as the rotational imprint beyond shape.

  • Added from the revised parts: the relaxation boundary and the pairs of stars, with GPT’s own test by direction (Parts 9 and 16); falling and holding as different acts (Part 15); clocks with their internal order changed (Part 18); shading at alignments (Part 15); the five tests of rhythms and redistribution (Part 13); the re-reading of existing data; and what would count against GPT for each.
