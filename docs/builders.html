The Plan to End Poverty · 2025 edition

Ending poverty by 2050, by design.

We already have the people and the skills. What we run out of is the budget line. The Plan to End Poverty is a designed system, built on three instruments that pay for essential work, give everyone a path to stability, and connect every citizen to the services they need.

This is not charity. This is structure.

Three instruments, one system

Each one does a single job. Together they make poverty unnecessary.

Instrument 1 · 7 papers

Civic Credits

Pay for the work a community needs

A public unit of value created only after verified work for the community: a nurse's shift, a teacher's lesson, a crew restoring a park. Credits arrive in the worker's wallet the next morning and spend like money at shops, landlords and utilities.

Explore Civic Credits → Instrument 2 · 6 papers

Poverty Elimination Centers

A complete path from struggle to stability

Campuses that bring housing, meals, healthcare, counselling, education, job training and real paid work under one roof, for anyone facing homelessness, unemployment or long-term hardship. Joining is a choice.

Explore Poverty Elimination Centers → Instrument 3 · 32 papers

LifeLink

One link to every public service

A secure digital identity and wallet on a free tablet, app or wearable. It connects each person to their Civic Credits, learning, healthcare, emergency help and a direct voice in government.

What it looks like on an ordinary day

From the plan's public lecture, Ending Poverty by 2050.

A day in the lifeA nurse, evening shiftSix hours at a PEC clinic and two hours of community triage, verified by her supervisor. Credits arrive overnight at the full billed value of nursing work, and she uses them at the supermarket, for childcare and for transit.
A day in the lifeA teacher, after-school literacyThree afternoons a week. Credits are paid weekly after verification, and rent is paid straight from her wallet, with the landlord settled automatically.
A day in the lifeA city horticulture workerA full-time civic role keeping parks planted and streets clean. It is work cities once called too expensive; now it is paid in credits that cover food, housing and utilities.
A day in the lifeA solar-installer apprenticeOn-the-job hours are verified by a licensed mentor. He earns at the apprentice rate, eats at the PEC cafeteria, and his transit pass is covered.

Implementation roadmap

The road to 2050

Five phases with three review gates, so each step is audited before the next begins. These are the targets set in the 2025 edition.

  1. 2026–2030
    Pilot and validationPilots in 6 to 8 early-adopter countries; 100 Poverty Elimination Centers; 2 million LifeLink tablets and 5 million wearables; Civic Credits for health, education and local infrastructure; independent evaluations published. Review gate in 2030.
  2. 2031–2035
    National scale-upFull national coverage in the first-wave countries; 10,000 new centres; 100 million devices made locally; Civic Credits connected to national payment systems; 1 billion people enrolled. Review gate in 2035.
  3. 2036–2040
    Regional integrationRegional councils align credit values, LifeLink ID and centre standards, so education and health credentials transfer across borders; 3 billion people on connected networks by 2039.
  4. 2041–2047
    Global interconnectionA universal Civic Credit standard and an exchange for international value transfer, connected to global aid, trade and environmental systems. Review gate in 2045.
  5. 2048–2050
    Institutional maturityGovernance made permanent and independent; the framework self-sustaining; poverty ended as a structural condition.

The full roadmap, with country selection and participation pathways, is in Chapter 9: Implementation.

Is it possible?

Every part already works somewhere.

Nothing in the plan needs a technology or an institution the world has never seen. What is new is putting the proven parts together, under one set of rules, aimed at one goal.

The design also keeps the risks small. Credits exist only after work is done. They run on the payment systems shops already use. The pilots start with one or two sectors in a region, publish their results, and pass a review gate before anything grows.

What isn't proven yet is the whole system working as one. That is exactly what the pilots are for.

  • Paying for public workIndia has guaranteed rural households paid public work since 2005: 100 days a year under MGNREGA, raised to 125 days by the law that replaced it in December 2025.What it shows: Civic employment can run alongside private jobs, at national scale.
  • Sharing resource wealthThe Alaska Permanent Fund has paid eligible residents a yearly dividend from oil revenue since 1982. University of Alaska researchers estimate it lifted about 25,000 Alaskans out of poverty in 2015, about a third of the poverty that would otherwise have existed.What it shows: Public ownership of natural wealth can fund people directly.
  • A parallel currencySwitzerland's WIR has let small and medium businesses trade in their own complementary currency since 1934; more than 60,000 firms took part by the 2010s. Research finds its use rises when unemployment rises, steadying the economy in downturns.What it shows: A second currency can circulate alongside national money, and help most when times are hard.
  • Money on a phoneA study in Science found Kenya's M-Pesa mobile money lifted about 194,000 households, 2% of the country, out of extreme poverty from 2008, with the largest gains in households headed by women.What it shows: Digital wallets can reach everyone, and change lives when they do.
  • Rewarding verified effortBrazil's Bolsa Família pays families who keep children in school and attend health check-ups. By 2013 it reached about 50 million people for around 0.6% of GDP, and Brazil's IPEA found it cut extreme poverty by about a quarter.What it shows: Payments tied to verified actions change lives at national scale, cheaply.

Questions people ask

Is People of the World political?

No. It is structural and moral, not partisan. The plan is written so that any government, of any party, could pilot it.

Do Civic Credits replace money?

No. They run alongside national currency at one-to-one parity, on the same card terminals and online payment systems. Choosing to work for credits is voluntary for workers and employers, and either can switch back to ordinary wages.

Do businesses have to accept them?

Using credits is a choice; being able to spend them is built in. Governments take credits for taxes, fees and public services, payment networks carry them beside cards, and a national rollout has registered businesses accept them at parity, so a credit always spends.

Isn't this just printing money?

No. A credit is created only after real work has been verified, so new spending power appears at the same moment as new services: more nurses at work, more teachers in classrooms, more homes repaired.

Where does the value come from?

From three anchors: the work already done, governments accepting credits for taxes and fees, and merchants being able to exchange credits for dollars at parity.

Who pays for it?

No single source. Credits are created against work, so they need no upfront budget; what needs funding is the reserve behind the cash-out guarantee and the centres themselves. The plan draws on several layers: revenue from natural resources and public utilities, the taxes and fees paid in credits, the centres' own enterprises, public and development-bank investment, and partners. Each country sets its mix, and it adjusts as conditions change.

Does everyone have to join a PEC?

No. PECs are for people who need help or want a structured path forward.

Do I have to pay for LifeLink?

No. LifeLink is provided to every citizen, as an app on a phone you already have or as a free device.

Has it been tried?

Not as a whole system yet; that is what the pilots are for. Each of its parts has precedents that already work, from public employment guarantees to resource dividends and mobile money.
