# Introduction – People of the World: Ending Poverty by 2050

*The Plan to End Poverty*

By Shareef Ali Rashada

Growth itself has decoupled from human advancement. The 2020s mark the weakest global-growth decade since the 1960s. Debt distress, automation, ecological constraints, and geopolitical shocks have eroded the assumption that GDP expansion alone can lift the poor.6 Profit-centered production deepens inequality. Businesses, pressured to minimize costs and maximize shareholder return, suppress labor compensation and externalize social cost. Essential work—care, maintenance, local service—remains undone because fair payment reduces margins. This asymmetry ensures that inequality grows faster than output, even in years of prosperity.7 Implication A credible 2050 strategy must move beyond the poverty line and the growth model that reproduces it. It must build parallel systems of value creation that reward contribution and service directly, not only those activities profitable to capital. Civic Credits, PECs, and Lifelink Tablets together constitute this new circulatory system—where human capacity, not scarcity, becomes the organizing principle of economy.

By 2022, despite decades of development effort, approximately 8.5% of the global population—nearly 700 million people—were estimated to live on less than US\$2.15 per day in 2017 PPP terms. Even at broader thresholds, deprivation remains vast: in 2022 an estimated 26% (1.85 billion people) lived on less than US\$3.65 per day, while nearly 46% (3.71 billion people) survived on less than US\$6.85 per day.8 Labor markets remain fragile: as of 2019, the informal economy accounted for roughly 58% of global employment—meaning that over half of all working persons lack formal protections or social insurance.9 Growth alone has not been sufficient. Poverty reduction has nearly stalled, with absolute numbers increasing in regions such as sub-Saharan Africa and in fragile or conflict-affected states. Over two-thirds of those in extreme poverty now reside in such environments.10 Taken together, these facts indicate that poverty is not a simple matter of low income, but a systemic outcome of structures that generate insecure labor, fragmented services, unor under-rewarded social contribution, and chronic underinvestment in capability formation.

Therefore, a credible 2050 strategy must go beyond growth-plus-transfers and instead construct systems in which:

1\. Value for work and service is decoupled from private profit; 2. Labor and community contributions are measured, verified, and rewarded; 3. Identity, connectivity, and service access are universal; 4. The previously excluded are integrated into the circulatory mechanisms of economy and society— not relegated to residual charity.

In short, the elimination of poverty demands not merely improving the bottom decile, but transforming the entire architecture of value so that no human being remains outside the flow of recognition, service, and participation.

Global Poverty Indicators — Summary Extreme Poverty: As of 2022, 8.5% of the global population—around 700 million people—lived on less than US\$2.15 per day (2017 PPP). Most of these individuals reside in Sub-Saharan Africa, which accounts for more than 60% of the global total.

Near-Poor Populations: Roughly 1.85 billion people (26%) survive on less than US\$3.65 per day, while 3.71 billion (46%) live below US\$6.85 per day. These thresholds represent the working poor—people in employment but still trapped in deprivation.

Informal Employment: Informality remains the dominant mode of work, covering 58% of the global labor force and up to 90% in Sub-Saharan Africa. Such workers lack stable income, insurance, or bargaining power.

Inequality: The global Gini index stands near 0.68 for market income (0.55 after transfers). The top 10% hold 76% of total wealth, and the top 1% alone hold 38%.

Fiscal Constraints: Over half of low-income countries are in or near debt distress, limiting fiscal space for investment. Global growth averages 2.8%—the weakest decade since the 1960s—while labor’s share of GDP has fallen from 55% to 43%.

Youth and Gender Gaps: One in four young people (282 million) are not in education, employment, or training. Women earn roughly 20% less than men worldwide, with gaps exceeding 30% in developing regions.

Structural Insight: Poverty lines understate real vulnerability. More than half of humanity earns below US\$10 per day, while income volatility, informality, and inequitable growth perpetuate systemic fragility. A global 2050 plan must therefore address structure, not symptoms.
