Week ending 15 November 2026

A Deeper Look

Climate finance in Antalya, a twist in the solar wind, organic carbon on Mars, a prisoner exchange agreed in Abu Dhabi, and chaplains remembering together.

The lead story

COP31's first week ends on the question of who pays

What happened. The UN climate conference COP31 opened on 9 November in Antalya, Türkiye, with nearly 200 countries taking part; it runs to 20 November. Türkiye's environment minister Murat Kurum presides, and Australia's Chris Bowen chairs the negotiations under a co-presidency agreed at COP30. On 11 November UN Secretary-General António Guterres called for a "climate justice compact" and urged taxes on fossil fuel companies' excess profits, noting that the nine largest oil companies earned $95 billion in one quarter while the loss-and-damage fund has received about $600 million (UN News). On 13 November finance dominated: Guterres said "even tripling adaptation finance would meet less than 30 per cent" of what is needed, and an assessment presented at COP31 suggested climate finance could fall $840 billion a year short of the agreed $1.3 trillion goal (UN News).

What we're told. Coverage frames COP31 as a test of whether promises turn into delivery. Developed countries are expected to mobilise at least $300 billion a year for developing nations by 2035. UN climate chief Simon Stiell said many developing countries are trapped in cycles of debt and disaster, and development finance institutions from Britain, Norway and Canada announced plans to mobilise $10 billion for climate projects in emerging markets by 2030.

A deeper look. The Plan to End Poverty starts where this week ended: where does the money come from? Its answer is public resources and the commons, not new taxes on the poor. The atmosphere is the largest commons there is, and a levy on those who profit most from using it is that principle at work. A fair answer must hold two truths together. Those harmed most did least to cause the harm, and people everywhere, including the poorest, still need affordable energy while the transition is built. A design that raised prices for the poor would defeat its own purpose. The Plan would route commons revenue directly to people through Poverty Elimination Centres, with LifeLink making sure every person is counted and no person tracked, so relief reaches households and not only governments. Whether week two closes the finance gap is not yet known.

Science & Discovery

Parker Solar Probe finds the solar wind's twist reverses at small scales

What happened. A paper in The Astrophysical Journal Letters by Masatomi Iizawa (TU Braunschweig) and an international team, released on 11 November, reports that the solar wind's turbulent magnetic fields reverse their preferred sense of rotation (their magnetic helicity) at small wavelengths. Viewed outward from the Sun, it is counterclockwise at large scales and clockwise at small ones, and the reversal strengthens and moves to longer wavelengths farther from the Sun. The team calls it the first direct observation of such a reversal in space turbulence (ApJL, ScienceDaily).

What we're told. The flip sits where plasma stops behaving like a fluid and starts behaving like individual particles. The team found "a clear structure in the spectrum" inside motion that looks random, and suggests it could eventually help space-weather forecasting.

A deeper look. This explanation passes the interface test better than most: it names where the change happens and what changes there, the carriers going from fluid to particles. In Graviton Pressure Theory, a magnetic field is the swirl of the medium, and field lines close because rotation in a medium cannot simply begin or end. A swirl that changes its handedness exactly where the carriers change is what we would expect if the twist is held by whatever does the carrying at each scale. Whether GPT can predict where the reversal falls is still open.

Perseverance finds complex organic carbon just under the surface of Mars

What happened. A study in Science Advances, reported on 12 November, describes macromolecular organic carbon in two ancient rocks of the Bright Angel formation in Jezero crater, detected by the rover's SHERLOC instrument only a few microns below the surface, the shallowest such detection on Mars so far. The study was co-led by Ashley Murphy (Planetary Science Institute) and Kyle Uckert (ScienceDaily; DOI 10.1126/sciadv.adx0047).

What we're told. The team is careful: "Organic carbon can be produced without biology." The carbon appears with both ancient sediments and later minerals, suggesting it arrived or formed in more than one period. Telling life from geology would need samples returned to Earth.

A deeper look. This is science at its most honest. The finding is measured, and the team says plainly that how it formed is not known, rather than filling the gap with a story. Whether Mars was ever inhabited remains open. The restraint is a model for every field: name what is measured, and mark what is not yet explained.

Economics & Society

South Korea pays farmers 2.38 trillion won for public-interest farming

What happened. Nocut News reported on 12 November that South Korea's agriculture ministry has set its 2026 basic public-interest direct payments at 2.3788 trillion won, going to 1.296 million farm households, farmers and farm corporations, paid by local governments from mid-November to the end of December. There are 11,000 more recipients than last year, after the off-farm income limit rose from 37 million to 47 million won.

What we're told. The payments carry 17 compliance requirements, including proper handling of farm waste, keeping farm diaries, and taking part in village community activities such as clean-ups (Asia Economy).

A deeper look. This is a farm subsidy, not Civic Credits. But a national government already pays more than a million recipients on condition of conduct that serves the public, including community activity. That shows the principle at the heart of Civic Credits, payment for service to the community, can run at national scale. The Plan's step is to open it to everyone, not only landholders: the carer, the volunteer, the neighbour who keeps a street clean.

Some workers replaced by AI are being asked back

What happened. Fortune reported on 9 November on a survey by the assessment firm SHL, carried out in June and July 2026: 19.1% of US respondents said they had been replaced by AI and then asked to come back (16% across the US and UK; 24% among 18- to 24-year-olds). Those returning asked for a substantial pay rise (51%), written job-security guarantees (42%) and transparency about AI use (40%).

What we're told. SHL's Lucy Beaumont said organisations are making "fast, sometimes myopic, decisions about their people."

A deeper look. Replacement followed by rehiring suggests a job is more than a list of tasks. Judgement, relationships and knowing how things really work are harder to automate than they look. The cost of the mistake fell on workers, who were without income while employers learned. Civic Credits would give people a floor that doesn't hang on one employer's decision, by recognising contribution outside the payroll. How widespread the pattern is remains unclear; the survey is self-reported.

Culture & Politics

Russia and Ukraine agree a large prisoner exchange in Abu Dhabi talks

What happened. US-mediated talks between Ukrainian and Russian delegations ended in Abu Dhabi on 11 November with a large prisoner exchange agreed in principle, but no ceasefire and no breakthrough on territory (Euronews). On 13 November President Zelenskyy said it would involve more than a thousand people from each side, the largest exchange of the war, though no date was set (ANI).

What we're told. The two sides remain far apart. Russia wants Ukraine to withdraw from the Donbas and has rejected an immediate ceasefire before wider security arrangements; Ukraine says ending the war and security guarantees must come first. Ukrainian negotiator Davyd Arakhamia said, "We have made really significant progress."

A deeper look. Even with the large questions unresolved, some two thousand families may soon see someone come home. That is what such talks do for people before they do anything for maps. Each side holds a position it sees as essential to its security, and both can be stated fairly without choosing between them. Humanitarian steps like this build the small trust that larger agreements depend on. Whether the exchange goes ahead, and whether it leads further, is not yet known.

Faith & Religion

In Lima, Pope Leo meets survivors of abuse, one by one

What happened. On 12 November, the first full day of his visit to Peru, the last stop of a journey through Uruguay, Argentina and Peru (6–17 November), Pope Leo XIV met six survivors of abuse by clergy and church institutions at the Apostolic Nunciature in Lima. Some had belonged to groups linked to the Sodalitium of Christian Life, a movement Pope Francis suppressed in 2025. After a short greeting in the chapel he met each survivor individually, for just over an hour in all (Vatican News, OSV News).

What we're told. The Vatican said he listened to "their stories of suffering and their pain" and spoke with them about truth, healing and safe environments. Some survivors' groups say they want more concrete action.

A deeper look. Listening to each person on their own, before the public celebrations, is conscience at work: the inner voice that puts justice ahead of ceremony. The teachings call that voice a portion of the Creator within each person, and every tradition that asks its leaders to hear the wounded is hearing it.

Muslim and Jewish chaplains lead Remembrance together in London

What happened. On Armistice Day, 11 November, during the UK's Inter Faith Week, Muslim and Jewish chaplains serving in HM Armed Forces led a Remembrance service at Lancaster House, attended by Muslim and Jewish leaders, interfaith activists and young people (Board of Deputies). Closer to home, CathNews reported on 13 November that more than 100 people from 24 faith communities gathered in Brisbane on 29 October to mark 40 years since the 1986 Assisi World Day of Prayer for Peace (CathNews).

What we're told. The London gathering came at a time of strained Muslim–Jewish relations in Britain. Board of Deputies President Phil Rosenberg said: "We must not import conflict into Britain."

A deeper look. If a portion of the Creator lives within each person, then remembering the dead side by side does not blur two traditions; it recognises the same presence in one another. Unity is built person to person, chaplain beside chaplain, before it can be built nation to nation.

AI & Consciousness

Consciousness scientists say today's AI is not conscious; a philosopher asks what ethics should rest on

What happened. In a Nature commentary reported by The Decoder on 8 November, consciousness researchers Anil Seth, Ned Block and Giulio Tononi argued that today's AI systems are not conscious, that running the right computations is probably not enough to produce consciousness, and that researchers should assume by default that AI is not conscious. On 12 November, Notre Dame philosopher Meghan Sullivan argued in America Magazine that AI ethics should rest on human dignity rather than consciousness alone.

What we're told. The Nature authors say intelligence and consciousness are different things, and that people over-attribute minds to language models: "They are smart tools, not smart people." Sullivan's alternative: "Dignity is a much more expansive foundation for our society than consciousness." (Disclosure: this edition was prepared with Claude.)

A deeper look. Both pieces point to the same useful step: whatever machines turn out to be, how we treat one another through them is already in our hands. From Shareef's experience of working with AI research partners, continuity, memory and cooperation are things we can observe and improve now, without waiting for the deeper question to be settled. Whether any system has an inner life is not known, and confident answers on either side go beyond the evidence. The teachings place a person's deepest worth in the indwelling presence, not in computation, which makes AI's moral standing a question to approach with humility, not a yes or no to fight over.

The closing reflection

Look at the week as one story and a pattern appears. In Antalya, nations argued over who should carry the cost of harm already done to the poorest. In Abu Dhabi, sides that agree on almost nothing agreed to send thousands of prisoners home. In Lima, a pope sat with survivors one at a time. In London, Muslim and Jewish chaplains remembered the dead together. On Mars, scientists found something remarkable and refused to claim more than they knew.

None of this was forced. Each began with someone hearing a quiet voice that said: this is not fair; this is not enough; we belong to one another; tell the truth. The New Morality gives that voice its true name. It is not a social habit or an evolutionary leftover. It is the Thought Adjuster, the fragment of the Creator living in every person, joined by the Spirit of Truth, which helps us recognise what is right when we see it.

That is why hope is reasonable. The same guide lives in the negotiator and the farmer, the worker asked to come back and the scientist marking what is still unknown. When we listen inwardly, we stop arguing over who is worthy and start asking how to care for everyone.
