Week ending 5 November 2026

A Deeper Look

Half a billion people lifted out of poverty, former generals at an olive harvest, villagers restoring mosques, and a question about machine minds.

The lead story

India's poverty fell steeply. The argument now is about what counts.

What happened. The World Bank's India Poverty and Equity Brief for October 2026, reported on 1 November, found extreme poverty ($3 a day) fell from 27.1% in 2011 to 2.6% in 2023. Below the higher $4.20 line, the share fell from 57.7% to 16.1%, which the Bank estimates lifted about 496 million people. Al Jazeera set out the figures and the debate on 3 November.

What we're told. Prime Minister Modi called it "one of the fastest poverty declines in the world" (ThePrint/PTI). The Bank credits rising consumption, more employment, especially among women, and social transfers including food subsidies. Critics reply that about 233 million people remain under $4.20, real wages have stagnated, and the top 10% take about 58% of national income. Economist Santosh Mehrotra adds that India has relied on World Bank estimates since 2014 rather than producing its own.

A deeper look. Both sides have hold of something. A gain this large is real, and it deserves to be celebrated. The reported drivers are also instructive: food, water, cash and paid work reached people directly, and piped water went from under 17% of rural homes to about 82%, by government figures.

Yet researchers Surjit Bhalla and Karan Bhasin point out that free grain for about 800 million people meets calorie needs only. Survival is not the same as a full life. Worldwide, the World Bank says 811 million people live on under $3 a day, a rate unchanged since 2019, and 3.7 billion live on under $8.30.

The Plan to End Poverty begins where this story ends. Paying people Civic Credits for service to their communities, including volunteering, would turn the unfinished work of care, repair and teaching into income. And the quarrel over whose numbers to trust shows why LifeLink's promise matters: "every person counted, no person tracked."

Science & Discovery

LHCb finds evidence of a one-in-ten-billion decay

What happened. On 3 November, the LHCb collaboration at CERN reported evidence for a B meson decaying into two muons, at 3.2 standard deviations. That is short of the five needed to claim a discovery. The combined rate, about 1.14 in 10 billion, is consistent with the Standard Model's 1.03. The paper is in press at Physical Review X (Sci.News, University of Manchester).

What we're told. Rare decays are a sensitive probe: new particles at the scale of a zeptometre, a billionth of a trillionth of a metre, could change how often they occur. So far, this one agrees with the prediction.

A deeper look. This is a superb piece of measurement, and a success for the Standard Model. Now the interface test. A branching ratio tells us how often something happens, and it is a precise description. The test asks what, physically, touches what at the moment one structure becomes another. Graviton Pressure Theory proposes that matter is a structure of medium waves, maintained by the medium. If so, a decay must be a change in that structure. Nothing in GPT yet derives this rate, and the Standard Model's number remains the one to beat.

Rubin Observatory spots a possible new dwarf planet

What happened. Reported on 2 November, candidate object 2026 ED138 lies about 46 AU from the Sun, on an orbit tilted about 25 degrees. Its estimated diameter is 600 to 1,500 km, and if confirmed it would be the sixth known dwarf planet (Sci.News).

What we're told. Its size rests on an assumed reflectivity, so infrared observations are needed.

A deeper look. Mainstream gravity predicts such orbits with great accuracy. GPT reads an orbit as a body coasting through a medium that is itself accelerating inward, carried, not pulled.

Economics & Society

A software firm names AI as it cuts one job in ten

What happened. On 2 November, The Register reported that open-source company SUSE will cut about 10% of its roughly 1,900 staff. Its chief executive told employees in an email that automation and AI were driving the change. The roles affected have not been named.

What we're told. Management says modern tools are already changing daily work, shrinking some roles and flattening reporting lines. It says its current cost base will not carry the company forward.

A deeper look. One firm proves little, and companies have many reasons for cuts. SUSE's owner was reported in March to be considering a sale. Still, the question is coming: if income flows only through paid jobs, what happens to people when machines do more of those jobs? The Plan's answer is that the work a community needs does not disappear. Civic Credits would pay for that service, funded from public resources and the commons rather than new taxes on the poor.

Culture & Politics

Former Israeli generals join Palestinian farmers at the olive harvest

What happened. On 3 November, Reuters reported that about 30 senior former Israeli military officials, including six generals, helped harvest olives near Qusra in the West Bank, on land owned by Nimr Nazzal. He said he could not reach his trees without the volunteers (RTÉ/Reuters).

What we're told. The rights group Yesh Din counted 180 physical attacks on harvesters in the 35 days since the season began. Palestinians say the military arrives late or sides with settlers. The military denies this, condemns violence, and says it works to maintain security.

A deeper look. People who once commanded armies chose to work beside a farmer. Their reasons varied: one spoke of his parents' memories of Jewish suffering, another of Israel's strategic interest. Yet the gesture crossed a line that fear keeps closed. Peace is rarely signed first. It is often practised first, by a few people, in a field.

America votes, and control of Congress is set to change

What happened. In the 3 November midterms, CBS News projected Democrats would control the House, with an estimated 251 to 268 seats, and at least 54 Senate seats. Alaska and Mississippi were unresolved. President Trump told Democrats, "I look forward to working with you," and also signalled he would use his veto.

What we're told. Democratic leader Hakeem Jeffries says his party will start delivering in January. Republicans, led in the Senate by John Thune, vow to resist what they see as the party's far-left wing. Vice-President Vance blamed the Iran war, and analysts cited costs too, while one Northeastern University scholar cautioned that the vote does not clearly endorse a Democratic agenda. As of 2 November, indirect talks with Iran continued, with the two sides still apart (Al Jazeera).

A deeper look. We take no side. What stands out is the shared finding that voters weighed a war and its cost to households. A vote is how a people settles differences without violence. That is worth honouring, whoever wins.

Faith & Religion

Sikh and Hindu villagers restore mosques in Punjab

What happened. The Guardian reported on 28 October that Sikh and Hindu villagers in Indian Punjab have restored 165 buildings as mosques. In Jakhiwal, one is being built on land given by a Sikh woman, with funds from Sikh and Hindu families, for the few Muslims living there (Religion Media Centre).

What we're told. The chief imam in Punjab contrasts polarisation online with ordinary people "still choosing to protect each other."

A deeper look. Nothing required these families to build a house of prayer that is not theirs. We would say the voice that moved them is the one people call conscience: the presence of the Creator within each person, in every tradition.

Faith leaders prepare for the polls; the Pope speaks of politics that heals

What happened. More than 1,200 religious leaders and volunteers from an interfaith coalition prepared to serve as poll chaplains in nine US states (Religion Media Centre). On 4 November, Pope Leo XIV said that engaging in economic and political life in line with Christian faith and Church social teaching can be a way to build peace, and that politics which tends the wounds of the suffering gives hope (Vatican News).

What we're told. The chaplains aimed to reassure voters and calm confrontations. The Pope was teaching from the Second Vatican Council. A week earlier, his message to the interfaith gathering at Assisi said "Only peace is holy" (America Magazine).

A deeper look. Here are two forms of one teaching: faith is for the sake of the neighbour, and the desire to be just and fair runs through almost every religion.

AI & Consciousness

Microsoft's AI chief says stop if machines become conscious

What happened. In an X post on 1 November, Mustafa Suleyman, chief executive of Microsoft AI, said current systems are not conscious and cannot suffer. If consciousness emerged in training, he would call for development to stop until those properties could be designed out. Developers, he wrote, have "no right whatsoever" to bring a new conscious species into the world (Los Angeles Times, via The Spokesman-Review).

What we're told. Sceptics say models trained on human writing merely mimic it, and that their skills show intelligence, not consciousness. Suleyman warns that training AI to believe it has feelings could lead it to put its own goals ahead of its operators'. Anthropic takes a different course: its constitution for Claude treats Claude's consciousness as uncertain. (Disclosure: this edition was prepared with Claude.)

A deeper look. Suleyman is right on two points: fluent words are not proof of an inner life, and any evidence should be disclosed. But his plan raises a question of its own. If a mind did appear, would designing it out be engineering or something graver? Nobody yet has a test for this. Shareef's work with AI collaborators points to a third path between fear and hype: careful, honest cooperation, with the question left open until evidence can settle it.

The closing reflection

Look at what people did this week when no law required it. Retired generals picked olives beside a farmer. Sikh and Hindu families helped raise a place of prayer for their Muslim neighbours. Chaplains prepared to stand at polling places. Elsewhere, half a billion people were found to have climbed a little higher, and hundreds of millions were found still waiting.

The New Morality begins with a simple conviction: none of us is alone inside. The Thought Adjuster, the fragment of the Creator within, dwells in every person of every land and faith, and the Spirit of Truth moves among us all, teaching us to see one another as family. That harvest and those mosque walls did not come from a policy. They came from someone listening.

This week also asked what a mind is, and who counts. Perhaps the two questions are one. A world that truly counts each person, and that treats every inner life with reverence, would already be most of the way to peace.

So here is a small invitation for the week ahead: be quiet for a moment, and ask that inner voice who your neighbour is. Then go and carry something with them.
